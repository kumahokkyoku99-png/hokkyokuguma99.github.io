# Chapter Ten: _Altitude_
## I. Söğütlüçeşme

**Tuesday, March 11. 12:40.**

Kübra had dropped a spare key and a bag of dry food with Selin the night before — "she pretends to complain and secretly adores having a cat for four days" — and Berrin had watched them leave that morning from the third-floor windowsill with the specific indifference of an animal who had never once worried about being abandoned and had so far always been right not to.

On the platform, Charlotte was still failing at the station name.

"Sö-üt—"

"Söğütlüçeşme," Kübra said, unhurried, the way you'd correct a child you liked.

"That's what I said."

Erika said the whole word once, flatly, correctly, like a chemical formula read off a label. Kübra stared at her.

"That was perfect."

"I practised in the bathroom this morning."

"Least romantic possible response to good Turkish." Kübra picked up her bag. "Platform two."

---

## II. Istanbul Toward Ankara — A Book, and a Grandfather Twice Over

The 13:00 train pulled out on time, and within forty minutes the city let go of them.

Wide reclining seats, a screen tracking their speed — 243, then 251 — a trolley with tea and foil-wrapped biscuits. Charlotte watched the steppe blur past and thought, not for the first time since Warsaw, that speed like this made a place easier to admire and harder to actually see.

Kübra had a paperback resting spine-up on her knee, cover down, the way people hold books they're not sure they want to be asked about.

"What's that," Charlotte said.

Kübra turned it over without quite offering it. English translation, spine cracked, clearly reread. "Livaneli. _Bliss._" She hesitated. "I brought it for the route. It runs this exact road — Van to Istanbul, though the other direction from us. A girl from a village near the lake we're going to. Something terrible is done to her, and the village decides the correct response is for her to die for it rather than the man who did it. A soldier is sent to escort her west instead."

"That sounds extraordinary. Can I read it after you?"

Kübra didn't answer right away, and the not-answering was its own kind of answer.

"I keep going back and forth about whether to hand it to you at all," she said finally. "Not because it's badly written. Because of what it will do in your head before you've seen a single kilometre of the place it's set in." She turned the book over once. "You should know something about the man who wrote it, first. Livaneli was arrested during the same coup my grandfather lost his position over — nineteen seventy-one. He was younger, a musician then more than a novelist, and they held him for months and then he fled the country on false papers rather than wait to find out what came next. He spent twelve years outside Turkey before he could come back. Stockholm, Paris, Athens, New York — a decade of his life spent describing a country he wasn't allowed to stand in."

"So he wrote this from memory," Erika said. "Not from being there."

"He wrote it after he came back, decades later, so he did eventually stand in it again — unlike my grandfather's letters that nobody answered." Kübra looked at the cover a moment. "But you understand why I carry it carefully. My grandfather corrected grammar in a taxi for eleven years because a government tried to erase him quietly. This man was arrested outright and ran, and came back twelve years later to write a novel about a girl from near Van being marched toward a fate she didn't choose. More or less the same generation, punished by the same event. One of them gave the region its most famous novel. The other gave me his stubbornness. I don't know how to hold a book like that lightly."

Charlotte was quiet for a moment. "Then I want to read it more, not less. Knowing that."

"That's the answer I was hoping for and also slightly afraid of," Kübra said. "Because now if it gives you the wrong idea about the whole region, that's partly my fault. I'll have handed you a man's exile along with his novel and called it a gift."

Erika, who'd been listening without looking up from her own notebook, was quiet for a moment.

"Can I ask you something about that," she said slowly. "You've told me twice now that this book and your grandfather come out of the same year, the same crackdown. Does that make it easier to trust the book, or harder? I'd have thought it would settle the question. It sounds like it's made the question worse instead."

"It's made it worse," Kübra admitted. "Because now it isn't just _a novel about the region._ It's written by someone who paid for his convictions the way my family paid for theirs, about a region he wasn't entirely from either, and I've spent this whole conversation trying to work out whether his year of suffering earned him the right to speak for people even further east than where he was arrested. I don't think there's a clean answer. I think that's just what it costs to hand someone a book instead of a photograph — you inherit the author's failures along with his courage. You can't take one without the other."

She held the book out at last, spine up.

"Read it," she said. "But read it as one true story about one particular horror, not a map of everywhere we're about to go. Tell me, when you finish, if the only thing left in your head is honour and violence. I'll give you three other books before Van if it is."

Charlotte took it carefully, the way she took most things that mattered.

"Deal."

---

Nobody spoke for a long time after that.

The land was already climbing — Charlotte felt it in her ears before she understood it in her eyes, a faint pressure that came and went with the gradient, the plateau lifting itself in increments too slow to watch happening. Wind turbines gave way to nothing at all: pale, treeless country running to a horizon with no vertical interruption in it, the sky doing more work than the ground. An hour went by. Then another. Erika had her own notebook open and wasn't writing in it. Kübra dozed, or pretended to. Charlotte read the first forty pages of _Bliss_ twice, not because she'd forgotten what was in them but because she wasn't sure yet what she was supposed to feel, and rereading felt safer than deciding.

By the time the land had climbed enough that her ears popped properly, they had stopped talking almost entirely, and none of them seemed to mind.

---

They reached Ankara a little after three — not a change of train, the same seats, but a real stop, long enough for a new set of passengers to board. Through the window, beyond the modern platforms, the old station stood in Art Deco stone from the 1930s, joined to the high-speed concourse by a long glass skybridge, two different centuries welded together and told to get along.

"The capital," Kübra said. "Everyone assumes it's Istanbul. It isn't. Istanbul is the argument. This is where the argument gets written down and filed."

"It doesn't feel like either," Charlotte said. "It feels like a big, serious city that isn't trying to impress anyone."

"That's exactly what it is. It administrates. It doesn't perform." Kübra watched the new passengers settle. "And it still isn't Sivas. It still isn't Muş. There's always a next ring out, and the next ring in always gets to feel hard done by."

The train pulled out at three-eleven. Ankara fell away into flat gold afternoon, and by the time they reached Sivas — 21:04, three minutes early — the light was long gone and the platform was bitterly cold.

---

## III. Sivas — Hasan

Two slim minarets stood above the rooftops two streets from the station, floodlit — the Çifte Minareli Medrese, thirteenth century, glimpsed but not, tonight, reachable.

They ate nearby — _Sivas köftesi_, plain and exceptional — and were back on the platform by half past ten. The board said the Van Gölü Ekspresi was expected at 23:18. It came in at 23:47, twenty-nine minutes late, and looked, when it finally arrived, like it belonged to a different century entirely from the one they'd stepped off six hours earlier.

Heavy blue-and-cream carriages, riveted rather than welded, amber corridor light, a dining car somewhere ahead still audibly working. They boarded a little before midnight.

"This is the other half," Kübra said, finding their couchette — already occupied by a man in his sixties, asleep sitting upright, a newspaper on his chest.

He woke as the train finally lurched into motion at 00:06, blinked at three foreign women filling the rest of the compartment, and beamed.

"Hasan," Kübra translated, after an exchange that went on rather longer than an introduction required. "He worked this line for thirty-one years. Signals, then dispatch, out of Malatya. Retired six years ago and still can't stop riding it whenever his knees allow."

Hasan produced a paper bag of lokum, insisted, and told them things through Kübra that nobody had asked for and everybody wanted — conscripts the line used to carry west in his father's time, migrant labour changing at Sivas for Germany with cardboard suitcases, money for new track that always seemed to run out exactly where the interesting scenery began.

"Ask him if he minds," Erika said. "That it's slow. Late already, before it's even begun."

Hasan answered before Kübra finished translating, understanding the shape of the question if not the words, and Kübra laughed.

"He says slow isn't the same as wrong. The fast train doesn't let you see who's crying at a platform, who's being met by their mother — it's already gone. This one waits. He likes that it waits."

By half past midnight he was asleep again.

---

## IV. The Stop

It happened a little after one, somewhere past Yıldızeli, in open country with no station and no lit building anywhere.

The train simply stopped. An announcement came over a failing speaker, three sentences in Turkish, half lost to static.

"What did it say," Charlotte asked.

"Something about the line ahead," Kübra said. "Maintenance, or a fault. I got 'delay' and a number that might have been minutes or kilometres."

An hour passed. Frost spread from the corners of the glass inward. Hasan didn't wake — this had happened to him before, many times, and there was nothing left to feel about it that hadn't already been felt and filed away.

They ended up in the corridor.

"I don't fully trust where I am," Erika said eventually, to the window. "Not you, not Hasan, not anyone who's been kind to us today. People, individually, I trust. But the country, as an idea — I am passing through it because it's on the map between where we started and where we need to be, and some part of me has held it at arm's length all day. I know Turkey today isn't Turkey in 1915. Knowing it intellectually hasn't fully reached the part of me that flinched, a little, every time a soldier walked through this carriage."

Kübra didn't reassure her.

"Thank you for not pretending," she said. "You're allowed to hold the people and the flag at different distances. I do it myself, about my own country, every day."

"That doesn't make it comfortable."

"No. It isn't supposed to be. That's the point of accuracy."

The train moved again at 02:20. Nobody ever learned why it had stopped.

---

## V. Malatya, at Dawn

They came into Malatya a little after six, the sky going from black to grey to a thin hard blue, apricot orchards laid out bare-branched across a wide valley.

"Somewhere out there is a mound six thousand years old," Kübra said. "We won't see it. But it's there, under all of this, the way everything is."

A short stop only. A vendor worked the platform with simit and boiled eggs; Erika bought three without being asked.

---

## VI. The Dining Car

By mid-morning, hunger overcame the couchette, and the three of them walked forward through three carriages to the dining car — fourteen tables, most of them occupied, a smell of onions and simmering stock, a waiter moving between tables with the unhurried confidence of a man who had done this on this exact train for longer than some of his passengers had been alive.

They shared a table with an older couple heading to Elazığ and a solitary man who said little and read a newspaper folded into quarters.

The soup, when it came, prompted the first small friction — the older woman called it one name, the waiter, setting it down, used another, and neither corrected the other, as though the dish had simply always had two names depending on which side of the table you sat.

"Same soup," Kübra said quietly, catching Charlotte's look. "Different word for it depending on your grandmother. Nobody at this table is going to argue about which one is correct. They'll just both keep using theirs."

Halfway through the meal, a gendarme came through checking identification — the same unhurried, uninterested pass they'd seen the day before, table by table rather than carriage by carriage this time, close enough that Charlotte watched it happen at hand's length rather than through a compartment door.

He looked at three foreign passports with mild curiosity. He looked at Kübra's Turkish ID with none at all. He looked, for perhaps two seconds longer, at something on the solitary man's identity card — a place of birth, maybe, or a name — before handing it back without comment and moving to the next table.

Nobody at the table reacted. The man went back to his newspaper. The older woman went back to her soup.

Charlotte, watching, understood that she had just seen the same five-second act performed four different times at one table, and that it had meant four different things, and that only one of those meanings had been _nothing at all_ — and that the person for whom it had meant the least was her.

The man with the newspaper noticed, eventually, the cover of the book on the table by Charlotte's elbow.

"_Mutluluk,_" he said, nodding at it — the Turkish title. Then, in careful English: "Good book. Made trouble, when it came out. Some people very angry. Some people finally saw themselves written down." He returned to his paper before Charlotte could ask which he had been.

---

## VII. Elazığ, and a Second Book

By midday the land had narrowed further, the Euphrates glimpsed once as a wide brown ribbon far below a bridge, then gone. Elazığ came and went with a castle visible on a hill above the town.

"Harput," Kübra said. "And there's a church up there too, older than the castle. Nobody restores that one with the same enthusiasm."

Erika was looking at it for longer than the sentence seemed to warrant.

"I read a book before we left Paris," she said, a little awkwardly. "William Dalrymple. _From the Holy Mountain_ — he retraces an old monk's journey through the surviving Christian communities of the Middle East. Most of it isn't even set here. But there's a chapter on this region, monasteries that used to hold hundreds of monks and now hold four, elderly, waiting to be the last one." She shrugged, almost apologetic. "I didn't think I'd ever actually be where the chapter was about. I read it because Charlotte was researching Iran and I wanted something for the metro. And now there's a church on a hill and I recognise the _shape_ of it from a book, without knowing the first true thing about it that isn't secondhand."

"That's not nothing," Kübra said.

"It doesn't feel like enough. It feels like carrying a photograph instead of the place." Erika watched the castle slide past. "I don't know the alternative, at this speed. I just wanted to say it before the feeling passed."

---

## VIII. Bingöl, Muş

A young woman got on near Bingöl and took the last free berth — a duffel bag, immediately on her phone, the total unavailability of someone who'd done this exact commute many times.

Kübra tried, carefully, an hour later. "Are you going home?"

"Muş. Nursing, in Malatya." A pause, economical rather than hostile. "You're not from here."

"Istanbul. I photograph old buildings." Kübra hesitated. "I've read about the peace process this year. I wondered what it feels like, being from here, rather than reading about it from a train."

The young woman looked at her a moment — the patience of someone asked some version of this before, by people who meant well.

"We've heard 'peace' before," she said. "I'll believe it the day my brother stops having to show his ID four times between here and Muş instead of six. It's better than it was. I'm not going to pretend it isn't. I'm also not going to hand you a nice sentence to put in a caption."

"I wasn't asking for a caption."

"I know," the young woman said, softening by a small, exact degree. "That's why I answered at all."

A family boarded not long after — a mother, three children, coach class rather than couchette. An hour on, the mother appeared at their compartment door with a small paper parcel of simit, pressed it on them before anyone could object, and left before they could properly thank her.

Charlotte didn't write anything down for a long time. When she finally did: _A family travelling upright for a day and a half gave us bread. We are in a paid compartment with beds. I don't have a sentence that makes this feel like anything other than what it is._

---

## IX. Tatvan, Late

They were due into Tatvan at 13:44. They arrived at 17:02.

"We have lost three hours and eighteen minutes since Sivas," Erika said, checking against the schedule she'd screenshotted in Istanbul, "not counting the twenty-nine we'd already lost before we boarded. This isn't a train that runs late occasionally. Its lateness is one of its stated dimensions, the way its length is."

"Welcome to the reputation," Kübra said. "Everyone tells you before you go. Nobody quite believes it until they're standing here at five instead of two."

---

## X. The Pier

The ferry had no posted time. A man at the ticket office said it left "when it left" — three crossings a day, timed loosely against whichever trains needed meeting on either shore. He shrugged in a way that suggested he'd stopped finding this remarkable sometime in the 1990s.

They waited on a bench, the lake enormous and silver-still, mountains holding the last sun on their upper slopes long after the water had gone dark.

"I finished the first half," Charlotte said, to Kübra, holding up the book. "I don't think it gave me the wrong idea. I think it gave me one true idea, and today gave me four or five others — the soup with two names, that man's face when he saw the cover, the nurse who wouldn't hand you a sentence, Hasan and his lokum, a family giving us bread from a seat they'll be sitting in for another day. I don't know how to file all of that in the same drawer. Some of it's beautiful. Some of it's the thing your book warned me about. It's all true at once and I don't know what to do with that."

"Don't file it," Kübra said. "That's the whole answer. The filing is the tourist's instinct — one clean drawer, one label. You're not required to resolve it into a single idea about a whole region. You're only required to have actually seen what you saw."

"I keep thinking about my sister," Kübra went on, more quietly, unprompted this time. "Everything I'd hand you was written by someone who left, or someone who visits. Livaneli went. Shafak lives half in London. I train in for a project and train back out to a flat with hot water and a cat someone else is feeding right now. My sister left properly — no project, no complicated relationship to staying — Berlin, and she stopped explaining Turkey to anyone, including herself. Some days I think that was the more honest choice. We don't talk. I think every time we do, I can feel her thinking my staying is a performance, and I can feel myself thinking her leaving was an abandonment, and neither of us has found the sentence that isn't an accusation dressed as an observation."

"What were you born as," Erika said, "and how do you face it. That's what we've circled for two days. You didn't choose this project any more than I chose my name or Charlotte chose her blankness. Your sister inherited the same thing and put it down anyway. Neither of you chose the inheritance. You only chose what to do with it."

"Tidy sentence for a bench in the dark."

"I'm good at those. It's usually how I survive the ones that aren't."

"I don't know if I'll call her when I get back," Kübra said. "I'm not going to promise it just because the moment wants a promise."

"Then don't," Charlotte said. "Say you're still deciding. That's allowed to be the whole answer."

"Still deciding," Kübra repeated, testing the weight of it. "Yes. That's the honest one."

The boat came into view a few minutes later — not the one they'd been told to expect. They boarded a little after half past seven.

---

## XI. The Crossing

The lake at night was black glass, colder than anything since Warsaw — a dry, mineral cold rising off the water itself.

Near the pier, and again further out where the searchlight swept the surface, there was ice — not a sheet, but broken plates of it, pale and slow, grinding faintly against the hull. Erika stood at the rail longer than the cold should have allowed.

"I didn't know lakes this far south iced."

"Sixteen hundred metres," Erika said. "It isn't about south. It's about up."

Far out, low and barely there, Kübra thought she could make out a darker shape against the stars. "Akdamar," she said, quietly, not lifting the camera. Nothing here that film would keep. "I'll come back for it properly. When it's actually a place and not just a guess."

---

## XII. Van

They came alongside the pier a few minutes past midnight, the thirteenth of March now, though none of them had noticed the date change until Erika said it aloud.

Above the town, for only a few seconds, a long dark ridge broke the skyline where the last traces of the day's light had no business still existing. Not a building yet. Just an absence of sky where sky should have been.

"Van Kalesi," Kübra said, not lifting the camera. "You won't see it properly tonight."

"Three thousand years old, and I get a silhouette after midnight."

"And it can wait one more night for you to look at it properly. It's the one thing on this whole route that's never had to hurry."

Charlotte let the shape go, unresolved, into the dark behind them.

She wrote, later, with what little charge her phone had left:

_"Twenty-nine minutes late leaving Sivas. Three hours and eighteen more lost by Tatvan. A ferry with no schedule at all. We stopped once for no reason anyone would tell us and waited once more for a boat with no announced time, and both waits held the heaviest things anyone said all day._

_A man in a dining car recognised the book on the table and told me it made trouble when it came out, and some people finally saw themselves written down, and then went back to his newspaper before I could ask which one he'd been. A soldier checked four sets of papers at our table and it meant nothing to me and something to someone else and nobody said which. A soup had two names and nobody argued._

_Erika said she doesn't fully trust the ground she's standing on, and Kübra didn't argue her out of it. Kübra doesn't know if she'll call her sister, and said 'still deciding,' which was the bravest thing anyone said all day, because it was the only one of us who didn't reach for an answer just to have one._

_I crossed a region today that I did not understand before I arrived, and I don't think I understand it now, and for the first time on this whole trip I don't think that not-understanding is a failure. I think it might be the only honest thing to feel about a place you passed through once, at speed, as a guest._

_Tomorrow we rest. The fortress will still be there, not hurrying, the way it never has."_

# Chapter Eleven: _Alphabet_

## Part One: The Castle

---

**Wednesday, March 13. Van. Morning.**

The fortress had waited for them the way Kübra had promised it would — three thousand years unhurried, and one more night had cost it nothing.

They walked up from the town in clear, hard morning light, the ridge of black volcanic rock rising ahead of them with the old walls following its contour rather than fighting it, the way a good coat follows a shoulder. Below, on the flat ground between the fortress and the lake, lay the low, broken outline of what had once been the old city — Eski Van, mostly gone now, mostly grass and stone footings and a scattering of walls that stopped at waist height and simply didn't continue.

Kübra didn't photograph the ruins directly. She stood at the base of the mound for a long moment, camera lowered, and said only, "That was a city," in a voice that didn't invite a follow-up question, and none of them asked one.

The climb up into the fortress itself took twenty minutes, switchbacking along a path worn smooth by several thousand years of people wanting to see what could be seen from the top.

---

They found the inscriptions on the eastern face, cut into a smoothed panel of the rock — long, narrow, orderly rows of wedge-shaped marks, weathered but still legible as _marks_, if not as meaning.

"Urartian," Kübra said. "Ninth, eighth century before Christ. The kingdom that built the first version of this whole fortress, before anyone here spoke Turkish, or Kurdish, or Armenian, or Persian, or anything any of us would recognise."

Charlotte crouched in front of the panel, close enough that her shadow fell across the lower lines.

"Can anyone read it?"

"Scholars, some of it. Not fluently, not the way you'd read a newspaper. There isn't enough of it left in the world — a few hundred inscriptions, most of them short, most of them royal boasting. _I, king of kings, built this. I, king of kings, conquered that._ It took much longer to decipher than Egyptian or the cuneiform further south, because there simply wasn't enough surviving text to triangulate the grammar properly. A whole kingdom, and what's left of its voice could fit in a few library shelves."

"It's the same script, though," Erika said, examining the wedge shapes more closely. "The technique. Reed stylus, wet clay or cut stone. I read that cuneiform was borrowed and adapted for dozens of different languages across the whole region for thousands of years — Sumerian, Akkadian, Hittite, this. The _shape_ travelled even when the words underneath it didn't."

"A script outliving every language it was ever used to write," Kübra said. "That's rather the whole morning in one sentence."

Charlotte reached out, not quite touching the stone, tracing the shape of one wedge-mark a few centimetres above its surface.

"Somebody carved this thinking it would be read," she said. "Not admired. Read. Like a letter, or a record. And now it's just — beautiful. A shape people photograph. The actual sentence inside it is almost gone."

"Almost," Kübra said. "Not entirely. That's the part I find bearable about it."

---

They stayed on the walls a long time after that, looking out — the lake enormous and pale blue in full daylight, nothing at all like the black ice-fringed water they'd crossed at midnight; Süphan's snow catching the sun to the north; the modern town spread below in ordinary rooftops and satellite dishes and washing lines, going about its Wednesday entirely unconcerned with the three thousand years standing over it.

Charlotte was quiet for a long time, and when she finally spoke it wasn't to either of them exactly.

"I want to learn some Persian," she said. "Before we cross. Not enough to actually speak it — I know I don't have time for that. Just the letters. Enough to recognise a word on a sign, or a menu, or a name. I keep thinking about that—" she nodded back at the panel "—a whole language that people spent centuries trying to piece back together because too little of it survived. I don't want to arrive in a country and let its whole alphabet stay a shape to me the way that one just did. Even a little would be different from nothing."

"That's a good instinct," Erika said. "Also completely impractical on a two-day timeline."

"I know it's impractical. I'm doing it anyway."

"I didn't say don't. I said it's impractical. Those aren't the same objection." Erika almost smiled. "I'll help you with the alphabet tonight if you want. It isn't large — thirty-two letters, most of them cousins of Arabic script. It won't make you fluent. It might let you read one word on a sign before Tehran, which is one word more than either of us has managed with anything east of Sofia."

Charlotte looked at her, surprised and pleased in roughly equal measure.

"You'll help me learn a language you're not even trying to learn yourself?"

"I already have six. I'm not adding a seventh under this kind of time pressure. But you asked for thirty-two shapes, not a language, and I can teach shapes." Erika shrugged, the gesture almost apologetic. "It's the same instinct as the tools in my desk drawer, if you want to be generous about it. I like giving someone the correct piece for the job, even if the job isn't mine."

Kübra, who had been listening with her camera finally still, said nothing for a moment, then: "Get a notebook page ready tonight. I'll write out a few common words myself before I go — the ones you'll actually need. _Bread. Water. Thank you._ Small enough to carry, useful enough to matter."

"Thank you," Charlotte said, and meant several things by it at once.

---

## Part Two: The Scarf

They found what they needed in a narrow shop two streets back from the citadel's approach road — a place that sold, without much ceremony, exactly the mixture of goods a city on the last approach to Iran had learned to keep in stock: hiking boots, phone cases, dried apricots, and a wall of folded scarves in every weight and colour a traveller might plausibly need for the border ahead.

Erika went straight to the plainest end of the wall — dark navy, dark grey, a deep bottle green, all of them heavy cotton, unpatterned, the kind of fabric that would hold a fold and not slip.

"This one," she said, within ninety seconds, holding up the navy. "Large enough to cover properly without constant readjustment. Cotton, not silk — silk looks better and behaves worse, it slides the moment you turn your head. Practical."

Charlotte, three steps down the wall, was holding something else entirely — a pale rose-gold scarf shot through with a fine, barely-there paisley print, the kind of thing that would have looked at home under the beret in the very first café in Paris.

"Erika. Look at this one."

"That's silk."

"It's beautiful."

"It's beautiful and it will not stay on your head through a border crossing, a train platform, and twelve hours of sitting upright." Erika didn't even come over to look at it properly. "You can buy that one too, for later, for somewhere it doesn't matter if it slips. But you need the navy first. The navy is the one that has to work."

Charlotte held the rose-gold one a moment longer, turning it in the light from the shop's single window, then set it down — not entirely, just moved it two scarves over, within reach, unresolved — and picked up a plain grey one instead, matching Erika's in everything but colour.

"You always pick the version that doesn't want anything from you," she said, not quite an accusation.

"And you always pick the version that wants to be looked at," Erika said, without looking up from folding her own scarf into a neat, testable square. "We've established this about each other four times already on this trip. I don't see why the headscarf should be the exception."

"It isn't an exception. I just wanted it on the record that I noticed the pretty one first."

"Noted," Erika said. "Now try wearing the boring one. We have a border tomorrow, and beauty gets a turn after we've crossed it safely, not before."

Kübra, leaning in the doorway with her arms crossed, watched Charlotte attempt to pin the grey scarf under her chin the way she'd seen it worn in a photograph, get it slightly wrong, and try again.

"Tilt your head down before you pin it, not after," Kübra said. "You're fighting gravity instead of using it." She didn't come over to fix it herself. She let Charlotte find it on her own, twice more, until it held.

"There," Charlotte said, finally, catching her reflection in the shop's small mirror — grey fabric framing a face she barely recognised for a second, not because it looked wrong, but because it looked like the start of something she hadn't done before.

"There," Erika agreed, already paying for both scarves at the counter before Charlotte could object, the transaction closed the way she closed most transactions — quickly, and before anyone else had quite finished feeling the moment.

## Part Three: Van, at Dusk and Into Nowruz

---

The market square near the citadel road had changed since morning, though nothing about it had technically closed or reopened — it had simply, gradually, over the course of an afternoon, tilted toward something.

Charlotte noticed it first as colour. Stalls that had sold, at midday, the ordinary business of a border town — dried apricots, phone cases, boots — had begun, by five o'clock, to sprout small green trays along their front edges: wheat and lentil sprouts, packed tight in shallow dishes, a fresh haze of green against all the dust-brown of the street.

"What's growing everywhere," she asked Kübra, who was photographing a stallholder arranging a pyramid of the little trays with real care, as though they were jewellery rather than sprouted grain.

"_Semeni_," Kübra said. "Kurdish word — you'll hear _sabzeh_ too, that's the Persian one, and you'll meet it properly on the other side of the border in a few days. Same idea in both languages, more or less. You grow it from seed in the two weeks before the new year and by the day itself it should be a full green cushion. It means the year turning over. New growth standing in for everything old being let go."

"It's already happening here. We haven't even crossed yet."

"Newroz," Kübra said, and there was a small, deliberate emphasis on the word that Charlotte caught without fully understanding. "It's the same festival, the same date, the same equinox — but here it isn't only a spring holiday. For a lot of Kurdish families it's also the day their grandparents remember a legend about a blacksmith who lit a fire on a mountain and broke a tyrant's grip, and every year since, the fires get lit again, on purpose, as a way of saying: we are still the people who tell this story, whatever else has been true about this decade or that one. You'll see the bonfires tonight, up on the higher ground outside town. They started building the wood for them two days ago."

Erika, examining a tray of sprouts with the same clinical interest she brought to everything, said: "So the same seven or eight days, celebrated by two different peoples for two different weights of reason, and we're walking through the first version tonight and the second version sometime next week."

"More or less," Kübra said. "Try not to flatten it into one festival wearing two hats. It travels the same calendar. It doesn't carry the same load everywhere it lands."

They bought two of the small green trays from the stallholder, who wrapped them with more care than the price seemed to warrant, and Kübra insisted, over Erika's practical objection about carrying live plants across a land border, that they take them anyway — as far as they'd survive, which turned out, in the end, to be about as far as the platform the next morning.

As the light went, fires did begin to show on the dark hills above the town — small at first, then larger, scattered rather than coordinated, each one its own small orange point against the coming night, and somewhere down in the streets, faint and carried on the wind, drums.

Charlotte wrote her last Turkish postcard sitting on the citadel road wall, the fires visible over the rooftops behind her, the pen moving fast because the light was nearly gone.

## Part Four: The Farewell

**Thursday, March 14. Van.**

They had the whole day, which turned out to be both a mercy and a slow kind of ache.

Erika spent the morning finishing what she'd started on the citadel wall — sitting with Charlotte at a café table near the market, working through the Persian alphabet letter by letter, testing her on the ones from the day before, adding new ones in small, disciplined batches. Kübra came and went, packing in the intervals, appearing occasionally to correct a pronunciation neither of the other two could have caught.

"Train's not until eight tonight," Kübra said, dropping into the third chair sometime past noon, "which means you have no excuse for rushing this. Do it properly or don't do it at all."

"That's very nearly hospitable of you," Erika said.

"I contain multitudes." Kübra picked up Charlotte's practice sheet, scanned it, made one small mark with her own pen — a correction to a letter's final form when it closed off a word rather than connecting forward — and set it back down without further comment.

The afternoon dissolved into errands that weren't really errands — a last walk along the lakefront, the water flat and pale under a high grey sky; a stop at the market, where the _semeni_ trays had multiplied since yesterday and the bonfire wood was stacked higher on the corner where men had been building it since morning; a coffee nobody quite finished because nobody wanted to be the one to say it was time to go back and pack properly.

By seven they were at the station, early, because Erika did not believe in any other way of being at a station.

---

The board showed the 20:00 departure clearly enough — **VAN → TABRİZ → TEHRAN**, no fanfare, the same understated confidence every train on this whole journey seemed to carry once you got far enough from a capital.

"I looked into this train's history," Erika said, mostly to fill the last twenty minutes, though Charlotte suspected she'd have said it regardless. "It used to run from Ankara all the way through, twenty years ago — comfortable, popular, apparently. Cancelled outright in twenty fifteen, security problems in the east. Then a smaller version came back in twenty eighteen — one old couchette car, once a week, barely a train at all. Improved a little in twenty nineteen. Then the pandemic took it again. And now, twice a week, since the ninth and tenth of this month." She looked at the departure board. "We left Warsaw on the sixth. This train did not exist again, in its current form, until three days after we'd already crossed into Poland. We are not simply lucky with a calendar. We are riding something that has been cancelled and rebuilt more times than most of the countries it passes through."

"That's either very reassuring or the opposite," Charlotte said.

"I've decided it's reassuring. A thing that keeps being cancelled and keeps coming back anyway is not a fragile thing. It's a stubborn one." Erika glanced at Kübra. "I believe you'd approve of that description."

"I invented that description," Kübra said. "Several days ago. On a roof."

The whistle sounded further down the platform — not their train yet, something else departing — and Kübra's face did the thing it had been carefully not doing all day.

"Right," she said. "Say the things you're going to say. I'm not good at long ones."

Erika went first, the way she had at the pier, though this time there was no ferry noise to half-cover the words.

"Thank you for the four questions I asked you on a video call, and for answering all of them, and for the ones I didn't ask that you answered anyway." She held out her hand, and this time Kübra took it properly, shook it once, formal and almost solemn, before pulling her in regardless. "You'll hear from me. I keep my word about correspondence. It's one of the few soft things about me that I've never bothered to harden."

"I noticed," Kübra said, into her shoulder. "It's why I trusted you by the second night instead of the fourth."

She let go, and turned to Charlotte, and this time didn't wait to be reached for.

"Go find out what the alphabet's for," she said again, the same words as the fortress, deliberately. "And when you write the book — because you're going to write the book, I've known that since the first night on the roof — don't make this region simple. Not simple-bad, not simple-beautiful either. Leave it exactly as complicated as it actually is. I'll know if you don't."

"I'll know too," Charlotte said. "I don't think I could make it simple now if I tried."

"Good. That's the whole point of the last four days, then. It worked."

The platform announcement came, Turkish first, then something that might have been Persian, clipped and unfamiliar. Erika was already lifting her bag.

Kübra held Charlotte a moment longer, then stepped back, both hands still on her shoulders, and said the last thing plainly, without softening it:

"I still don't know about my sister. I want you to know I haven't decided just because you're leaving and it would be a tidy thing to say I had."

"I know," Charlotte said. "I wasn't going to ask."

"I know you weren't. That's why I told you anyway."

They boarded. The doors closed with a pneumatic sigh. Through the glass, Kübra didn't wave — she stood, hands in her coat pockets, the way she'd stood watching the fires the night before, and held that stillness until the platform, and then the whole lit rectangle of the station, slid backward into the dark and was gone.

Charlotte didn't write anything for a long time after that. When she finally opened the notebook, she only managed: _Gone. I'll write the rest of this later, when it doesn't feel like admitting something._

## Part Five: The Border, at Night

The train ran west out of Van and then curved, and within the hour the last lights of the city had fallen away entirely, replaced by a darkness so complete that the window became, for practical purposes, a mirror — Charlotte's own tired face looking back at her instead of any landscape at all.

**Kapıköy, 21:51.**

The train stopped with the particular finality of a station that expected to be stopped at for a while. Announcements, Turkish only this time — no Kübra to render them into anything else — and the corridor filling with the shuffle of passengers standing, stretching, some of them lighting cigarettes on the platform in the cold.

"Turkish exit control," Erika said, checking her phone against the printed itinerary Zahra's office had sent weeks before. "An hour scheduled. We're not moving again until ten to eleven."

The formalities themselves took less time than the waiting around them — a Turkish officer working through the carriage with the same brisk, incurious efficiency they'd met at every crossing since Horgoš, an exit stamp pressed into two passports without a second glance, and then nothing to do but sit in a stopped train on a dark platform for the better part of an hour while other carriages were checked in turn.

"I keep thinking," Charlotte said, watching a cigarette ember move back and forth on the platform outside, "that this is the last hour either of us will be able to read a single sign around us. Every station until Tehran is going to be in a script I've had two days to learn ten letters of."

"You have the sheet."

"I have the sheet. I don't have the fluency. There's a difference, and I'm about to feel it."

Erika didn't offer reassurance. She simply reached over and, without being asked, tested her on three letters from memory, in the dark, by the platform's amber light, until the whistle went and the train began, at last, to move again.

---

They crossed into Iran a little after eleven, though nobody announced the exact moment, and nothing about the darkness on either side of the border changed to mark it.

**Razi, 00:34.**

The Iranian border station arrived in full night — cold, floodlit, a longer platform than Kapıköy's, more staff visible, the whole apparatus of a proper international frontier rather than a provincial crossing point.

"Everybody down," a conductor said, in English this time, the words careful and rehearsed rather than fluent. "Passport. Bag."

They stood in a queue inside a hall lit by the flat, unforgiving light particular to government buildings the world over, at half past midnight, exhausted in the specific way that made every minute feel longer than the clock could account for.

The officer who eventually took their documents was young, unhurried, and — as promised, as researched, as everyone who had ever made this crossing seemed to agree — genuinely, disarmingly kind about it. He looked at Erika's visa, checked it against something on a screen, asked two questions in careful English about their route and their length of stay, and processed the whole exchange with a warmth that felt, after the last hour's cold platform waiting, almost startling.

"Germany and—" he checked Charlotte's passport "—Cascadia. Long way. You come by train the whole way?"

"The whole way," Erika said. "Since Paris."

He looked up properly then, something like delight crossing his face. "No airplane? None?"

"None."

"That is," he searched for the word, "very serious travelling." He stamped both passports, unhurried, careful, and handed them back with both hands, a small courtesy Charlotte hadn't seen at any border since Warsaw. "Welcome to Iran. I hope the rest is easier than tonight."

It was 02:41 by the time the last carriage had cleared processing and the train began, finally, to move east again — the whole crossing, door to door, consuming very nearly five hours of the night.

"Border officials very friendly," Erika said, half to herself, checking something on her phone one last time before putting it away for good. "I read that exact phrase, verbatim, in three separate places before we left Istanbul. I did not fully believe it until now. I don't know why disbelief was my default position on that particular fact and not on any of the others."

"Because it's the one fact that goes against everything anyone at home would have told you," Charlotte said, already half-asleep against the window. "It's easier to believe the frightening version. Nobody has to adjust anything to believe that one."

Erika didn't answer. But she didn't disagree either, and Charlotte, drifting, understood that as its own kind of admission.

---

Somewhere in the deep dark past Razi, Erika — awake, checking the map on her phone by the last of its battery — said, quietly: "Lake Urmia should be out there now. On the left."

Charlotte opened her eyes enough to look. There was nothing to see — no pale flat expanse, no glint of water or salt, only the same unbroken black that had held since Van, occasionally interrupted by a single distant light that might have been a farmhouse or might have been nothing at all.

"We're going to miss it," she said.

"We already have. It's behind us now, or nearly." Erika put the phone away. "The book said the trains run overnight on this stretch. I thought that was simply a fact. I didn't understand until just now that it was also a small tragedy, mentioned in passing. An entire lake, and everyone who rides this line sleeps through it."

"Not everyone's asleep."

"No. But everyone's blind, which amounts to the same thing in the dark." Erika settled back. "I'll have to imagine it. I'm told it looks like salt more than water, this time of year. I'll hold that in my head instead, since it's all I'm going to get."

Charlotte closed her eyes again. Somewhere beyond the glass, unseen, a whole vanishing lake went past.

## Part Six: Tabriz, and the Long Day to Tehran

**06:17, Friday, March 15.**

The train slowed into Tabriz as the sky was doing its first grey work above a low range of hills to the south — Sahand, Erika said, checking, a dormant volcano with a ski resort on its flank, absurd and ordinary all at once, the kind of fact that made a place feel suddenly, defiantly normal.

They didn't leave the train. A short stop, a change of crew, a vendor working the platform with tea in small glasses and something wrapped in paper that turned out, when Charlotte finally worked up the courage to buy one, to be a flatbread still warm from somewhere close by.

By the time the sun was properly up, the light did what darkness hadn't allowed it to do all night — it let the country in.

"Look," Charlotte said, holding up her phone, the currency note she'd photographed in Van still saved on the screen. "I can read one of these now."

Erika leaned over. The numeral in question — a single curved figure, unlike anything either of them had grown up writing — sat at the corner of the note.

"Which one."

"Five. I think. Kübra's sheet had them at the bottom and I went through them twice in the dark at Razi instead of sleeping." Charlotte turned the phone toward the window light to see it better. "Even the numbers look completely different here. I hadn't thought about that until yesterday. Six languages between the two of us and neither of us had ever had to learn new numerals before."

"I've been doing the same thing," Erika admitted, faintly sheepish, an expression she wore so rarely it was almost worth photographing itself. "One through nine and a zero. Ten shapes. I told myself it wasn't a language, only arithmetic, so it wouldn't count as breaking my rule about not adding a seventh language under time pressure."

"Did it work? The self-deception?"

"Completely. I recommend it. Self-deception is underrated as a study technique."

They spent the morning like that — comparing notes, testing each other, watching the country assemble itself in full daylight for the first time: orchards giving way to open agricultural plain, snow still lying on distant peaks to the north, small towns with signs neither of them could read quickly but both, now, could occasionally recognise a letter within.

By early afternoon the land had begun to climb again, the plain folding into foothills, a haze of green appearing on slopes that had been bare an hour before.

"We're not there yet," Erika said, checking the time, "but we're closer than we've been to anything since Van. Six hours, roughly, if the schedule holds. Which, given the last twelve, I am no longer assuming it will."

It held, more or less. The afternoon light went long and gold across increasingly mountainous country, real peaks now, taller than anything since the fortress ridge above Van, and somewhere in the late afternoon Charlotte fell asleep against the window with the alphabet sheet still open on her knee, and Erika let her, and didn't wake her until the city had properly begun — sprawling, vast, mountains rising abruptly at its northern edge, the light going from gold to grey to the first scattered lights of evening.

**18:34. Tehran.**

The train came to a stop beneath a high, echoing shed of a station, and for a long moment neither of them moved.

"We're here," Erika said, finally, and there was something in her voice that hadn't been there at any other arrival on the whole journey — not relief exactly, something closer to disbelief that a plan built in a Warsaw hostel lobby nine days and a lifetime ago had actually, against every reasonable expectation, delivered them to precisely this platform, on precisely this evening.

Charlotte looked out at the crowd on the platform — hundreds of faces, none of them familiar, a whole unreadable country arranged around a woman in a dark blue headscarf who was standing very still near the barrier, scanning the windows of the arriving carriages with the particular intensity of someone looking for two specific people among all the others.

"I think that's her," Charlotte said.

They gathered their bags. The doors opened. Fourteen hours after Van, ten days after Warsaw, they stepped down into Tehran.
